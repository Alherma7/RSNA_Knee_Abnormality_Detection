RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection

A knee MRI model built in two and a half weeks, and an honest look at why the winners spent their time on labels while we spent ours on the model.

Built for Kaggle's RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection research code competition (2026), closed 2026-10-22. Source: github.com/Alherma7/RSNA_Knee_Abnormality_Detection.

The problem

Predict 12 findings per knee MRI study: ACL and MCL injury, medial and lateral meniscus tear, osteoarthritis in three compartments, effusion, synovitis, Baker's cyst, bone contusion and fracture. The metric is the mean ROC-AUC over the 12. Inference runs in an offline Kaggle notebook with a 9-hour limit on about 1,300 hidden studies. The training set has 4,407 studies, 24,371 series and about 570 GB of DICOM from 16 sites.

Three facts shaped the competition. We understood the first two early. We underrated the third.

  • Only 58 training studies have expert labels. Two MSK radiologists and an adjudicator graded them from the images, with a published rubric. The hidden test set was graded the same way.
  • The other 4,349 studies only have a free-text report, in nine languages, and the test set has no reports. A report can be a teacher at training time, never an input.
  • Report wording does not match the rubric. "Small effusion" is negative. A low-grade MCL sprain is negative. Synovitis is missing from about 80% of reports, and how often it is mentioned depends on the hospital, not the knee. A report-only labeler tops out at about 0.90 AUC against the experts, whoever builds it. The labels are the model's ceiling.

The final model

1. Series to slots. Each study's series are sorted into 6 slots (3 planes × fluid-sensitive or not) by a header probe ported from a public kernel. Slices are put in anatomical order by projecting ImagePositionPatient onto the slice normal. The old filename-based sort reversed 41 of 58 gold studies.
↓
2. Pixel cache. A reused public cache (stevenleehans): 130 mm crop, 224 px, 9 slices per slot at 35%, 50% and 65% of the stack, right knees mirrored. At test time the same decode runs live and is checked against the cache (13 of 15 gold studies bit-identical).
↓
3. 18 pseudo-slots. Each slot's 3 anchor groups (3 adjacent slices as RGB) become separate inputs: 18 images per study.
↓
4. Model. DINOv2-small with the last 6 blocks fine-tuned. Then one attention query per finding over the 18 pseudo-slots, masking the missing ones.
↓
5. Labels and folds. Trained on a published LLM report-label table (0.893 AUC vs. gold) plus the 58 gold studies. Folds are grouped by the union of scanner fingerprint and report template. The 4 fold models are averaged at inference.

The key finding: the labels were the competition

The write-ups agree on where the score came from. 7th place: "most of my gain came from the labels." 49th place: the label rounds were "the WHOLE game." 5th place: better labels were their largest step. Gains attributed to each lever in the write-ups, on the public leaderboard:

We had diagnosed the problem correctly on Aug 25: our notes record that report labels have a "structural ceiling" because experts read the images and reports are a different process. We then validated a public 0.893 table, recorded "our own LLM pass is unnecessary", and closed the label question for good. Every later experiment changed the model. The winners did the opposite: 4 to 14 label versions each, and models that changed little after the first weeks.

Two other levers explain most of the rest of the gap. Input geometry: every top solution read far more of the knee than our 18 triplets at 224 px. They used 32–48 slices per series or every slice, at 336–384 px, and whole-study inference. The hospital as a variable: sites differ 2.5–27× in how often their reports call a finding positive, and image models learn to recognise the site. A training-only per-site logit offset, dropped at test time, was 33rd place's largest single gain.

The hardware wall

The project stopped at the next step on the list: re-rendering the cache at 336 px. On Kaggle's free T4 (16 GB), our ViT model already used 6.7 GB at 224 px with a micro-batch of 8 studies. At 336 px, each image has 2.25× the tokens and attention costs grow faster than that, so the projection went over the safe limit. Each pilot would also have used about 5 of the 30 weekly GPU hours. The limit was real. But the write-ups show it was not what separated us from the medal zone, because the same wall had known ways around it:

LeverWho used itWhat it buys
Train on a sample of the study, infer on all of it49th: 32 triplets per study in training, 96–160 slices per series at inference; 22nd: 16 windows; 21st: up to 176Training memory is set by the sample, not the study. Inference runs one study at a time with no gradients
Mixed precision + gradient checkpointing21st (bf16, 24 GB card), 22nd, 7th (fp16 on 2×T4 at inference)Roughly halves activation memory; checkpointing trades compute for memory
Tiny batches + gradient accumulation21st: 1 study per step, 2 accumulation steps; 22nd: batch 2; 49th: 2 studies × 2 stepsThe batch size we were protecting (32) was not needed

What we did with the limit was stop. The project memory records a 336 px plan with a stated fallback (micro-batch 4 with 8 accumulation steps), and no run to check it. A hardware limit is an engineering problem with a known list of levers, or a bill to price. It is not a reason to end a competition with seven weeks left.

What this isn't: a story about compute. The 7th place trained each model on one GPU with no external data. The 49th place was a student on Kaggle and Colab. The 1st–4th places had not published write-ups when this was written. What the top finishers shared was procedural:

  • treat the training labels as the main thing to improve, and keep improving them all competition;
  • read as much of the knee as the 9-hour budget allows, at a resolution where a 1–3 mm tear survives;
  • model where the label came from (the hospital, the report's wording), not only what is in the image;
  • judge changes with instruments whose noise is smaller than the change.

Deep learning on images: what transfers

The technical lessons from this project and the eight write-ups, for any study made of several 2D series or a 3D volume (MRI, CT) with study-level labels.

Input geometry

LessonEvidence
Choose resolution from the smallest structure. A d mm feature needs a pixel pitch of d/2 mm or finer, and a ViT sees one token per patch. Fix the crop in mm first, then the pixels224 px over 130 mm ≈ 8 mm per DINOv2 token, too coarse for 1–3 mm tears. 336 px: +0.008 public (49th). 448 px: no further gain. Crops converged at 130–150 mm
Coverage before selection. Read most of every stack; train on a random sample of windows, infer on the whole studyWe read 3 central slices per series. Top teams read 88–96% of the stack or every slice. Whole-study inference: +0.009 on the expert set (49th)
2.5D, not 3D. Three neighbouring slices as RGB through a shared 2D ImageNet backbone; neighbours chosen by mm, not by indexNo top solution used a 3D network. A 3D through-plane branch added nothing (49th)
Anatomy-anchored crops. Segment bone, place landmarks, crop each compartment at ~0.2 mm/px as extra series5th: nnU-Net bone masks, +0.002–0.003 on a strong base. 22nd: femur landmarks from TotalSegmentator

Architecture

LessonEvidence
Don't pool small findings away. Per-finding attention over windows (attention-MIL), spatial attention pooling, feature-map cells as instances, or per-finding queries with cross-attentionA 3 mm tear is ~0.01% of a frame. 8th: spatial attention pooling; 33rd: ~12 mm cells with 3D coordinates; 7th and 49th: finding queries
Tell every token where it came from. Embeddings for plane, contrast, relative depth or 3D position in mmUsed by 7th, 22nd, 33rd and 49th; lets a sagittal and a coronal window at the same spot attend to each other
Block shortcuts structurally. One compartment at a time, shared weights, the other side mirrored22nd: the only fix for lateral-from-medial OA that worked; a post-hoc correction cost −0.007
Modest CNNs over big or foundation backbones. Spend effort on input and labels firstConvNeXt-T/S, EfficientNetV2, CoAtNet won. Bigger never won (our DINOv2-S→B: null). DINOv3 only tied after tuning its own LR. In-domain SimMIM: −0.008. OAI/RadImageNet pretraining: ≤ +0.001

Series type from the header, not the name. TE/TR, ScanOptions, inversion time, 2D/3D22nd: 13,046 of 24,371 series had no usable name, and names that could be checked were sometimes wrong

Training, ensembling and inference

LessonEvidence
Fit the GPU with known levers: sampled windows, bf16 + gradient checkpointing, 1–2 studies per step with accumulation, a CNN instead of a ViT, a frozen-backbone second stageSee the hardware wall. 21st, 22nd and 49th all trained at 336–384 px with batches of 1–2 studies
Ensemble different views, not seeds. Per-plane models, crop models, different labels; blend per finding on ranks22nd: seed or backbone swaps correlated > 0.95 with the base and moved nothing; plane and crop models did. 7th: four same-pipeline models added +0.001
Engineer inference against the clock. Decode once and share across models; a time guard per stage; per-file DICOM decoding in try/except with offline decoders5th: shared decoding saved 42% of GPU time. 21st: a three-crop submission timed out on the hidden set

Lessons

Find the supervision bottleneck, and reopen it every week

In a weak-label competition the labels are the model's ceiling. We measured our labels once (0.893), called the question closed, and spent every later day on the model. The test that reopens it is cheap: compare the model's AUC with its own labels' AUC on the reference set. While the model is below its labels, work on input and model. Once it catches up, the next gain is in the labels. Re-run that comparison after every model change, and keep at least one label experiment going at all times.

The public baseline is a floor, not a template

Reusing a public pixel cache was the right call for correctness: this project's own preprocessing had a bad track record. But the cache also carried the launch baseline's design choices (224 px, 6 slots, 3 central slices), and they became our ceiling. Reuse a public pipeline to get a correct first submission. Then decide on purpose which of its choices to keep, starting with input geometry: resolution, how much of each stack is read, which series.

Use the leaderboard as an instrument

Five submissions a day over two months is about 300 scored measurements on about 400 expert-graded studies, against 58 locally. We used three. Paired submissions that differ in one change, and the column-inversion probe for per-finding AUC, give answers the 58 gold studies cannot. Plan submissions in the experiment budget from day one.

Don't gate on an instrument whose noise is bigger than the effect

Two of our three fold-0 pilots came back inconclusive, as our own noise estimate said they would. Measure the instrument's noise first. Then pick an instrument that can detect the effect you expect: out-of-fold AUC on thousands of studies against the best labels available, a sealed fold, or the leaderboard. Fix the acceptance threshold before the run.

Match process weight to what is at stake

Spec, two independent reviews, a plan and subagent execution caught real bugs in the submission pipeline and the gate arithmetic. Applied to every exploratory pilot, it capped us at roughly one GPU experiment every two days. Use the heavy process for what ships (the inference notebook, the gate code) and a one-page notebook for a pilot that a single run will settle.

The last weeks are the competition, again

We stopped on Sep 1 with seven weeks left. The Kaggriculture retrospective (here) recorded the same lesson: it stopped on Sep 3, a month before its close. Two projects in a row is a pattern, not bad luck. Next time, plan the calendar backwards from the deadline and protect the final month for iteration.

Treat a hardware limit as a problem to solve, not a stop sign

The project closed when the next experiment did not fit in the T4's memory. Before stopping, go through the standard levers: train on a sample of slices and infer on all, mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, a batch of one with accumulation, a smaller CNN backbone, freezing the backbone. Then price the alternative: a rented 24 GB GPU costs well under a dollar an hour. If it still doesn't fit, choose the experiment that does. Only stop when the remaining options are written down and priced.

Model where the label came from

We used the site only to group folds and stop leakage. The winners also treated it as a cause of label noise: a training-only per-site term, or a per-site correction to the labels. When labels come from a process that varies by source (hospital, annotator, template), put that source in the model during training and remove it at test time.

What carried over well

Verifying forum claims on our own data before acting on them (slice order, label tables, scanner leakage). Measuring noise floors, which stopped a false ensemble win. Unit-tested src/ code, and a validation gate between the training cache and the live decode. The forum mining itself found every lever the winners used, except the image-reading labels. What was missing was speed, and the willingness to reopen a question we had marked closed.

Future: a late-submission rerun

The competition accepts late submissions, so each lever above can be measured against the real private split. Each stage changes one thing and has a gate.

1. Input. A whole-study 2.5D reader in the 49th-place style: every slice, 130 mm, 336 px, ConvNeXt-T, per-finding queries, whole-study inference. Same labels as A2 v2.
Gate: private score above 0.837. The 49th place got 0.898 public from its first one-fold model.
↓
2. Re-level the labels. Sort each (study, finding) into a report group (not mentioned, negative, mild, below rubric, meets rubric). Move each group toward its gold positive rate, with shrinkage.
Gate: a paired late submission, same model.
↓
3. Image-read labels. Start from a published image-aware label set (the 7th place released every label version), after checking its license. Then try our own multimodal read on the findings where reports fail most: synovitis, contusion, the OA compartments.
Gate: label AUC on gold-58, then a paired late submission.
↓
4. Teacher round. Labels = 0.3 text + 0.7 out-of-fold predictions, re-anchored to the text every round. Validate on a sealed fold the teacher never saw.
Gate: the sealed fold and the leaderboard agree.
↓
5. Site term. A per-site logit offset during training, dropped at inference. Pseudo-site = report language × scanner model × field strength.
Gate: paired late submission. Restoring the offset at test time should score lower.
↓
6. Write it up. Which levers moved the private score here, and how much, compared with the gains reported in the write-ups.

Stages 1, 2 and 5 fit the free Kaggle GPU quota. Stage 3's own reads need a multimodal LLM, but they can start from public label files.
